\begin{abstract}

A drone that loses GNSS after a flood must localize itself against imagery taken before the disaster. We ask when \emph{learning where to fly} pays off in this active localization problem. Following the Active Neural Localizer, we train PPO policies on the belief of a histogram filter, using real satellite imagery from before and after the 2024 floods in southern Brazil, and compare them with simple heuristics on paired episodes with hierarchical confidence intervals over seeds and map windows. With a weak descriptor, the policy matches a straight-line heuristic in success (\vOneSuccessPpoSTwo\% vs.\ \vOneSuccessStraightSTwo\%) while flying less, but it localizes falsely more often, and a preference-conditioned variant failed to control this risk. With a strong descriptor, perception does most of the work: random motion already succeeds in \vThreeTaqSTwoRandom\% of the episodes, and none of \vThreeMatrixRuns{} training runs passed our learning gate. In an exploratory experiment that adds controlled noise to the observations, the advantage of the policy over random motion grows from $\eAdvRandLZero$ to $\eAdvRandLThree$ percentage points, and most runs now pass the gate. A straight line flown at the highest altitude, however, degrades even less, and the gap of the policy to it widens from $\eAdvBestLZero$ to $\eAdvBestLThree$ points. Zero-shot transfer to the hardest real site shows the same ordering. Learning to act pays off against motion without a plan, but in our setting not against systematic coverage.
\end{abstract}
