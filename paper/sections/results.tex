\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{RQ1: Does learning to act pay off under ambiguous perception?}
\label{sec:rq1}
% Estudo v1, Taquari, teste sul, 1000 episódios pareados, argmax, 3 seeds da PPO. Tab. II.

In the v1 environment the descriptor is weak, and the choice of route matters. In S2 (map from October 2023), random motion succeeds in only \vOneSuccRandomSTwo\% of the test episodes, against \vOneSuccStraightSTwo\% for the straight line (Table~\ref{tab:v1}).
The PPO policy reaches \vOneSuccessPpoSTwo\% success. This is indistinguishable from the straight line: the hierarchical interval of the difference is $[\vOneDiffSuccessSTwoLow; +\vOneDiffSuccessSTwoHigh]$~p.p.
The policy is more \emph{efficient}. It needs fewer penalized steps (median \vOnePenStepsPpo{} vs.\ \vOnePenStepsStraight, Wilcoxon $p=\vOneWilcoxonP$) and flies about two thirds of the distance in its successful episodes (\vOneDistSuccessPpoSTwo{} vs.\ \vOneDistSuccessStraightSTwo~km).
It is also \emph{riskier}: \vOneFalseLocPpoSTwo\% false localizations against \vOneFalseLocStraightSTwo\%.
With the less outdated S1 map, the straight line is better than the policy (\vOneDiffPpoStraightSOne{} p.p., $[\vOneDiffPpoStraightSOneLow; \vOneDiffPpoStraightSOneHigh]$).
The learned policy clearly beats random motion, the lawnmower and greedy information gain in S2. It does not beat a heading-keeping rule that keeps reaching new terrain and never trusts the map.

\subsection{RQ2: What makes the problem hard, and does the policy transfer?}
\label{sec:rq2}
% v1: índice de mudança (F1, F2), zero-shot em POA, M1 não suportada. v3: baselines nos alvos (Fig. 2).

\textbf{Map change.}
Success falls with the map--observation change of the episode window, for every method (Fig.~\ref{fig:hard}a). From the least to the most changed quintile, the straight line drops from \vOneSuccessQOneStraight\% to \vOneSuccessQFiveStraight\% and the PPO from \vOneSuccessQOnePpo\% to \vOneSuccessQFivePpo\%. A color-difference covariate that does not depend on the descriptor points the same way (Spearman $\rho$ from \vOneRhoColorMin{} to \vOneRhoColorMax).
We had pre-specified a mechanism: the policy would be drawn to cells that look distinctive on the old map. It was \vOneMOneVerdict. False localizations do visit more changed cells (+\vOneMOneChangeDiff, CI $[\vOneMOneChangeDiffLow; \vOneMOneChangeDiffHigh]$), but these cells are neither more distinctive nor more changed than the cells visited by the straight line.

\textbf{Transfer.}
Applied zero-shot to Porto Alegre (map of February 2024), the S2 policy matches the straight line in the city center (\vOnePoaCenterPpo\% vs.\ \vOnePoaCenterStraight\%, CI $[\vOnePoaCenterDiffLow; +\vOnePoaCenterDiffHigh]$) and falls behind on the river islands (\vOnePoaIslandsPpo\% vs.\ \vOnePoaIslandsStraight\%, CI $[\vOnePoaIslandsDiffLow; +\vOnePoaIslandsDiffHigh]$).

\textbf{Which strategies survive ambiguity (v3).}
With the stronger v3 descriptor, the same heuristics separate into two regimes (Fig.~\ref{fig:hard}b, Appendix table). In Taquari every heuristic exceeds 83\%, and random motion alone reaches \vThreeTaqSTwoRandom\% (S2). In Canoas and Porto Alegre, random motion drops to \vThreeCanoasRandom\%, \vThreeIslandsRandom\% (islands) and \vThreeCenterRandom\% (center), while the straight line keeps \vThreeCanoasStraight\%, \vThreeIslandsStraight\% and \vThreeCenterStraight\%. Flying the straight line at 150~m helps where perception is hardest: \vThreeCanoasStraightHigh\% vs.\ \vThreeCanoasStraight\% in Canoas and \vThreeIslandsStraightHigh\% vs.\ \vThreeIslandsStraight\% on the islands.
Greedy information gain, which predicts observations from the outdated map, is the weakest informed strategy everywhere. In Canoas it falls below random motion (\vThreeCanoasGreedy\% vs.\ \vThreeCanoasRandom\%), and on the islands it only matches it (\vThreeIslandsGreedy\% vs.\ \vThreeIslandsRandom\%). Planning on what the old map predicts is the wrong inductive bias after a flood.

\subsection{RQ3: Can a preference-conditioned policy control the risk?}
\label{sec:rq3}
% Estudo v1, MOPPO (errata e results_mo_v1fix/). Linha curta; detalhes no apêndice.

Not with our budget. The conditioning gate, which checks at 25\% of training that different preference weights produce different behavior, had already failed during training. With greedy actions, the policy chose the same action in 100\% of the probed states for the ``uniform'', ``fast'' and ``safe'' presets, so the original grid evaluation reported identical numbers for 32 of 37 weights. Re-evaluated with sampled actions (the mode used in training), the presets differ only slightly. The pre-specified hypotheses on the response to the weights (\moHSixVerdict) and on fewer false localizations in Porto Alegre (\moHSevenVerdict) both failed, and the comparison against fixed-weight PPOs was \moHFourVerdict. Averaged over presets, the expected utility of the conditioned policy (\moEumSampled) is no better than that of the plain PPO seeds (\moEumPocSampled). We report this as a negative result: in this problem, the weight conditioning was too weak to steer the trade-off.

\subsection{RQ4: When does learning to act pay off?}
\label{sec:rq4}
% Números só de results_v3/E (make_numbers.py); vereditos só de results_v3/E/verdicts_E.json. E é exploratório (V46, V50–V53).

\textbf{A ceiling in the v3 environment.}
With the fine-tuned v3 descriptor, Taquari is easy: random motion already succeeds in \vThreeTaqSTwoRandom\% of the S2 test episodes and the straight line in \vThreeTaqSTwoStraight\%. Learning to act has almost nothing left to add. None of the \vThreeMatrixRuns{} PPO runs of the main v3 matrix (three variants, ten seeds each) passed the learning gate: their advantage over random motion at 25\% of training ranged from $\vThreeMatrixAdvMin$ to $\vThreeMatrixAdvMax$~p.p. Following our protocol, these runs produce no verdict on the pre-specified v3 hypotheses (Appendix).

\textbf{Experiment E: controlled ambiguity.}
To ask when learning to act pays off, we made perception ambiguous on purpose, keeping everything else fixed. We added Gaussian noise of scale $\sigma$ to the observation descriptors of Taquari (fold~A), never to the map, and retrained the same PPO at each level (five seeds, same budget). The levels were chosen from the success of random motion on \emph{training} windows only, before any policy was trained. Random success barely moves for $\sigma\le 3$, although the top-1 retrieval of the true cell falls from \eTopOneLowSigma\% ($\sigma=\eLowSigma$) to almost zero. The filter accumulates evidence over many steps, and the shape of the valid area and the window border carry information of their own. We therefore extended the grid (\eNSigmas{} values of $\sigma$) and used the three levels L1--L3 ($\sigma=\eSigmaLOne,\eSigmaLTwo,\eSigmaLThree$), with random success on training windows of \eRandTrainLOne, \eRandTrainLTwo{} and \eRandTrainLThree\% (L0, no noise: \eRandTrainLZero\%). The hardest level did not reach the 35\% we had aimed for. Experiment E is exploratory; all its runs were evaluated, whether or not they passed the gate.

\textbf{The policy learns to beat motion without a plan.}
As ambiguity grows, the advantage of the policy over random motion grows (Fig.~\ref{fig:e}, blue): from $\eAdvRandLZero$~p.p.\ $[\eAdvRandLZeroLow; \eAdvRandLZeroHigh]$ at L0 to $\eAdvRandLThree$ $[\eAdvRandLThreeLow; \eAdvRandLThreeHigh]$ at L3. Training also starts to work: \eGateLZero{} runs passed the gate at L0, \eGateLTwo{} at L2 and \eGateLThree{} at L3. In this sense, learning to act starts paying off exactly where perception stops doing the work.

\textbf{But the best heuristic gains even more.}
The best heuristic on training windows was the \eBestName{} at every level. It is almost insensitive to the noise: \eSuccBestLZero\% at L0 and \eSuccBestLThree\% at L3 (Table~\ref{tab:e}). The PPO falls from \eSuccPpoLZero\% to \eSuccPpoLThree\%, so its gap to the best heuristic widens from $\eAdvBestLZero$~p.p.\ $[\eAdvBestLZeroLow; \eAdvBestLZeroHigh]$ to $\eAdvBestLThree$ $[\eAdvBestLThreeLow; \eAdvBestLThreeHigh]$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:e}, orange). The pre-specified criterion E3, that the advantage over the best heuristic grows from L0 to L3, is therefore \eThreeVerdict: $\Delta(\mathrm{L3})-\Delta(\mathrm{L0})=\eThree$~p.p.\ $[\eThreeLow; \eThreeHigh]$ (argmax: $\eThreeArgmax$, $[\eThreeArgmaxLow; \eThreeArgmaxHigh]$). The comparison is not an artifact of altitude: the PPO flies at 100~m, and at L3 the straight line at 100~m (\eSuccStraightLThree\%) and the lawnmower (\eSuccLawnmowerLThree\%) also beat it, while greedy information gain is close to it (\eSuccGreedyLThree\%). Under ambiguity, the policy also takes no less risk than random motion (\eFalseLocPpoLThree\% vs.\ \eFalseLocRandLThree\% false localizations).

\textbf{Real ambiguity: zero-shot transfer to Canoas.}
We had planned to train and test the policy inside Canoas, the hardest real site, but its post-event image leaves only \eCanoasWindows{} valid windows, almost all in one corner, which is not enough to train and test on disjoint terrain. As a descriptive check, we evaluated the ten Taquari L0 policies zero-shot on all Canoas windows. They succeed in \eZsPpo\% of the episodes $[\eZsPpoLow; \eZsPpoHigh]$: above random motion ($\eZsDiffRandom$~p.p., $[\eZsDiffRandomLow; \eZsDiffRandomHigh]$) and greedy information gain ($\eZsDiffGreedy$, $[\eZsDiffGreedyLow; \eZsDiffGreedyHigh]$), level with the lawnmower ($\eZsDiffLawnmower$, $[\eZsDiffLawnmowerLow; \eZsDiffLawnmowerHigh]$), and below the straight line ($\eZsDiffStraight$, $[\eZsDiffStraightLow; \eZsDiffStraightHigh]$) and the straight line at 150~m ($\eZsDiffAltStraight$, $[\eZsDiffAltStraightLow; \eZsDiffAltStraightHigh]$). Real and synthetic ambiguity tell the same story.
